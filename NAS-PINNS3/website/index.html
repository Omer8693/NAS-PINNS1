Physical Problem — A356 Aluminium Water Quenching

An A356 aluminium casting is heated to 540°C then rapidly quenched in water, cooling toward 20°C. The governing equation is the transient heat equation ρCₙ ∂T/∂t = K∇²T with a Robin (convective) boundary condition K ∂T/∂n = h(T−T∞). Solving this repeatedly with FEM is expensive: each time step requires assembling and solving a large linear system. With 21 time steps and hundreds of casting runs, computation time dominates the design loop.

The centre temperature follows T(t) ≈ 20 + 520·exp(−1.75×10³·t). At t=5 s the centre is ~513°C; at t=30 s it has dropped to ~488°C. Accurate temperature prediction is critical because residual stress and permanent distortion depend strongly on the spatial gradient ∇T.

What is a PINN?

A Physics-Informed Neural Network encodes physical laws directly into the training loss. Instead of requiring labelled simulation data, the network is penalised for violating the PDE, initial condition, and boundary condition. Given inputs (t, x, y) the network outputs T̂(t,x,y). Training minimises ℒ = ℒₚₑₑ + ℒ⸺, + ℒ𝐢𝐶 where each term is computed by automatic differentiation through the network graph.

Key advantage: no expensive simulation data is needed during training. Once trained, inference is microseconds per evaluation — far cheaper than a FEM solve. The trade-off is that PINN accuracy depends critically on network capacity, which NAS optimises.

What is NAS and why does architecture matter?

Neural Architecture Search automates the choice of hidden layers, neurons per layer, and activation function. A network that is too small cannot represent the solution; one that is too large overfits the PDE collocation points and is slow to evaluate. Three optimisers are compared: Bayesian (TPE) uses a probabilistic surrogate model to guide search toward promising configurations; NSGA-II and NSGA-III are evolutionary algorithms that jointly minimise L2 error and parameter count.

Best result: Bayesian finds 5 hidden layers × 151 neurons with relu activations — 92,564 parameters, L2=0.076 at 500 epochs, improving to 0.030 after extended training (Level 5). Only Bayesian meets the L2<0.10 production target at 500 epochs.

Key Innovation — The Skip Operator

Rather than using the PINN as a standalone solver, we embed it inside the FEM time loop. The “skip operator” lets the PINN predict Tₖ₊ₛ from Tₖ directly, skipping s FEM solves. FEM only runs at anchor steps; PINN fills the gaps. With skip=4, FEM is called 6 times instead of 21 — a 4× reduction in the most expensive compute phase.

Surprisingly, at skip=2 the Bayesian PINN achieves ratio=0.76×, meaning the PINN-filled prediction is MORE accurate than no skipping. FEM accumulates small numerical errors at every step via truncation error; the PINN takes a direct “shortcut” from Tₖ to Tₖ₊₂ and avoids this accumulation. This is a key finding with practical implications for hybrid FEM-PINN schemes.

7-Level Framework
Each level progressively builds on the previous — from single-shot prediction to multi-domain generalisation. Levels 1–3 establish the core thermal pipeline; Levels 4–7 extend to mechanics, training improvement, auxiliary losses, and geometry generalisation.
L1

Single-Shot NAS-PINN

Global T(t,x,y) predictor trained end-to-end. NAS finds 5×151 relu for Bayesian — L2=0.076, the only optimiser meeting the <0.10 target at 500 epochs.

L2

Temporal Skip Operator

PINN predicts Tₖ₊ₛ from Tₖ, filling skipped FEM steps. skip=4 gives 4× speedup. Bayesian at skip=2 improves MAE (ratio=0.76×) due to FEM numerical error accumulation.

L3

Hybrid FEM+PINN Routing

PDE residual r decides FEM or PINN at each step. Threshold=0.10: Bayesian uses PINN for 81% of steps. Config B reaches 95% PINN — only 1 FEM call per run.

L4

Distortion Mechanics

PINN T(x,y) feeds plane-stress mechanical FEM. Thermal gradients → residual stress → permanent distortion. Bayesian: 1.67 mm vs measured 0.78 mm (47 CMM points).

L5

Extended Training

20k epochs + cosine LR scheduler. NSGA-II improves 4.5× (0.252→0.055), meeting target. Bayesian improves 2.5× (0.076→0.030). NSGA-III only 2× — architecture is bottleneck.

L6

Poisson Fine-Tuning

Auxiliary Poisson PDE loss as regulariser. Bayesian gains +17% L2 improvement. Marginal gains overall — architecture capacity remains the binding constraint.

L7

Multi-Domain NAS

NAS applied to 5 Poisson geometries: Square, Circle, Annulus, L-Shape, Flower. Circle L2=0.00014 (trivial). Flower L2=0.17 (hard). Demonstrates geometry generalisation.

Each level answers a different engineering question: L1 “Can a PINN learn the full thermal field?” → L2 “Can it skip FEM steps?” → L3 “Can it decide adaptively?” → L4 “Does thermal accuracy translate to mechanical accuracy?” → L5 “How much does training budget matter?” → L6 “Do auxiliary physics losses help?” → L7 “Does NAS generalise across geometry families?”

The framework is modular: L1 is a prerequisite for L2–L4, L5 re-runs L1 with extended training, L6 modifies L5 by adding an auxiliary loss, and L7 is an independent branch exploring geometry generalisation via Poisson domains.

Why three optimisers? Bayesian (TPE) uses a probabilistic surrogate model that predicts which region of the search space is most promising — it converges faster and more reliably into the low-L2 region. NSGA-II and NSGA-III are evolutionary algorithms that jointly optimise L2 error AND parameter count (multi-objective). They find smaller networks at the cost of more search evaluations and less consistency.

Key finding: Bayesian found 5×151 relu — deep but accurate (L2=0.076). NSGA-II found 3×153 tanh — shallower but nearly as many neurons (L2=0.252). NSGA-III found 3×75 tanh — smallest but least accurate (L2=0.513). Only Bayesian met the L2<0.10 target at 500 epochs. The convergence advantage of Bayesian TPE over evolutionary methods is consistent with the literature for low-dimensional NAS spaces.

Temporal Skip Operator — Level 2
PINN fills skipped FEM time steps. FEM only runs at anchor steps every s-th step.

The core innovation of this project. In standard FEM simulation, every time step requires solving a large linear system. The skip operator lets the PINN predict the temperature s steps ahead — from Tₖ directly to Tₖ₊ₛ — so FEM only needs to run at every s-th step. With skip=4, FEM is called 6 times out of 21 total steps (75% fewer), reducing compute time proportionally.

How it works mechanically: At anchor step k, FEM solves normally. The PINN then predicts Tₖ₊₁, Tₖ₊₂, ..., Tₖ₊ₛ−₁ from Tₖ as input. At step k+s, FEM re-anchors. This prevents PINN error accumulation over long chains — the FEM anchor acts as a correction mechanism every s steps.

Hybrid FEM+PINN Routing — Level 3
PDE residual decides at each step whether to use FEM or PINN. Drag the threshold slider.

How hybrid routing works. Unlike the fixed skip pattern in Level 2, the hybrid router decides at each step whether to use FEM or PINN. It computes the normalised PDE residual r = ||ρCₙ ∂T/∂t − K∇²T|| / ||ρCₙ ∂T/∂t||. When r exceeds the threshold, the physics are changing fast — FEM is needed. When r is small, the solution is smooth and the PINN prediction is trusted.

Advantage over fixed skip: The routing adapts to the physics. At t≈0 (rapid initial cooling) residuals are large, so FEM is always used. As the casting approaches steady state, residuals drop and PINN takes over. This means no accuracy penalty during the most dynamic phase.

FEM step (residual above threshold) PINN step (residual below threshold)

Interpreting the results. The residual is highest at t=0 (rapid initial cooling) and decreases as the casting approaches steady state. With threshold=0.10, only 4 of 21 steps use FEM for the Bayesian architecture — an 81% reduction. Higher threshold → more PINN usage (faster, but potentially less accurate); lower threshold → more FEM usage (safer, slower). The orange dashed line shows the current threshold; blue bars are FEM steps, green bars are PINN steps.

Configuration B (95% PINN): Setting threshold ≥0.18 for Bayesian architecture, the router calls FEM only once — at t=0. All subsequent 20 steps use PINN. This configuration gives the largest speedup but requires the Bayesian architecture quality to maintain accuracy. For NSGA-III, the residuals are uniformly higher (worse model), so more steps exceed the threshold and FEM is called more frequently even at higher thresholds.

Thermal Distortion — 47-Point CMM Comparison
PINN temperature field → plane-stress FEM → permanent casting distortion validated against CMM.

From temperature to distortion. The PINN temperature field T(x,y) is used as input to a plane-stress mechanical FEM model. Non-uniform cooling creates thermal strains ε = α·ΔT, which produce residual stresses and permanent distortion. After cooling, the actual casting shape is measured at 47 points using a Coordinate Measuring Machine (CMM) with sub-millimetre precision.

Why this matters: Dimensional accuracy is the primary engineering requirement in aluminium casting. A distortion of >1 mm typically exceeds tolerance for automotive structural components. The PINN-predicted distortion must match the FEM reference closely enough to serve as a drop-in replacement in the mechanical simulation pipeline.

Why PINN distortion is larger. The PINN thermal error (L2=0.076 for Bayesian) maps directly to mechanical error, but with amplification. The distortion calculation depends on temperature GRADIENTS ∇T, not absolute temperature values. A small L2 error in T can correspond to a large error in ∂T/∂x and ∂T/∂y, which drives the thermal strain ε = αΔT and the resulting distortion field. This gradient amplification is the primary reason even the best PINN (Bayesian) shows 2.23× distortion overshoot.

Path to improvement: After Level 5 extended training (Bayesian L2=0.030), both T and ∇T become more accurate simultaneously, and distortion accuracy improves significantly. The Level 4 result with L1 architectures should therefore be viewed as a worst-case bound; the distortion chain performs substantially better after L5 retraining.

Multi-Domain Poisson — Level 7
NAS applied to −∇²u = f across 5 geometry families. Colour = solution magnitude (viridis: dark blue → teal → yellow).

Why the Poisson equation? The Poisson equation −∇²u = f is the canonical elliptic PDE — it governs electrostatics, steady-state heat conduction, and fluid pressure. Testing NAS-PINNs on multiple Poisson domains reveals how architecture requirements change with geometry complexity. A network optimal for a smooth convex domain may be completely insufficient for a domain with re-entrant corners or oscillating boundaries.

Animates PINN prediction noise — simulates training uncertainty

Why does geometry matter so much? The PINN must learn both the PDE solution and the domain geometry simultaneously. Smooth convex boundaries (Circle) provide clean gradients; re-entrant corners (L-Shape) create solution singularities where ∇u is discontinuous; complex oscillating boundaries (Flower) introduce high-frequency features that standard MLPs with relu/tanh activations cannot represent without spectral encodings or sinusoidal activations (SIREN).

Annulus difficulty: Two concentric boundaries require the network to enforce Dirichlet conditions simultaneously on inner (r=0.3) and outer (r=1) circles. The solution varies smoothly between them but must satisfy both constraints simultaneously, requiring careful collocation point distribution near both boundaries.

Flower domain: The NAS selects a 6×192 relu network (222k parameters) but still achieves only L2=0.172, confirming the fundamental limitation of standard MLP activations for high-frequency boundary features. Fourier feature encodings (random Fourier features mapped to sin/cos) or SIREN (sinusoidal activations throughout) would significantly improve performance here — a natural direction for future work building on this framework.

Key takeaway: Architecture quality is the binding constraint throughout all levels. Bayesian TPE finds the superior architecture (5×151 relu) that meets the L2<0.10 target at 500 epochs and enables skip=4 (4× FEM speedup).
